1. Introduction  
Financial markets generate vast amounts of heterogeneous and dynamically 
evolving information. Market participants must continuously process textual news 
reports, analyst commentaries, earnings call transcripts, structured financial statements, 
numerical time-series data, and visual technical charts. The integration of these diverse 
modalities is essential for forming coherent market interpretations and making 
informed investment decisions. However, existing financial artificial intelligence (AI) 
systems remain largely fragmented, focusing on isolated modalities rather than holistic 
market understanding. 
Recent advances in multimodal large language models (MLLMs) [1] have 
demonstrated remarkable capabilities in integrating textual and visual inputs within 
unified representation spaces. (e.g. GPT -5.2[2]) These models c an synthesize cross -
modal signals and generate context-aware interpretations, making them promising tools 
for financial analytics. In parallel, retrieval -augmented generation (RAG) [3] 
techniques have been proposed to enhance factual grounding by dynamical ly 
incorporating external knowledge sources such as regulatory filings, macroeconomic 
reports, and historical disclosures. While these developments significantly improve 
multimodal information synthesis, most applications in finance remain limited to 
descriptive tasks such as document summarization, sentiment extraction, and question 
answering 
A fundamental gap persists between multimodal understanding and sequential 
financial decision-making. Financial markets are inherently dynamic systems in which 
decisions must be made under uncertainty, and actions influence subsequent states and 
reward distributions. Traditional financial AI approaches have addressed specific 
components of this process. Text -based models such as FinBERT [4] and 
FinancialBERT [5] have been applied to news and earnings calls for sentiment analysis. 
Time-series models, including LSTMs [6-8] and Transformer-based architectures, have 
been used for price forecasting. Vision -based models such as CNNs and Vision 
Transformers (ViTs)  [9-10] have been adopted for technical chart recognition. 
Reinforcement learning (RL) methods [11-14] have also been explored for algorithmic 
trading and portfolio allocation. However, these approaches are typically developed in 
isolation, lacking a unified architectu re that integrates multimodal perception with 
--- PAGE 2 ---
principled decision optimization. 
This study proposes a decision -centric financial AI framework that bridges 
multimodal market understanding and sequential policy learning. Specifically, we 
conceptualize multimodal large language models and retrieval-augmented mechanisms 
as market belief constructors . These components synthesize heterogeneous financial 
signals into structured latent representations that capture the semantic and contextual 
state of the market. Bu ilding upon these belief states, a reinforcement learning agent 
learns optimal, risk -sensitive policies for portfolio allocation and financial decision -
making. By placing RL at the core of the framework, the system moves beyond 
descriptive analytics toward actionable and adaptive decision support. 
In addition to decision optimization, transparency and accountability are critical 
requirements in financial applications. Unlike consumer-oriented AI systems, financial 
decision models operate in high-stakes environments where prediction errors may lead 
to substantial losses or systemic risks. Consequently, interpretability must extend 
beyond token -level attention maps to encompass decision -level attribution. This 
research integrates cross -modal attribution analys is to identify how textual signals, 
visual patterns, and retrieved evidence contribute to policy outputs. Such 
interpretability mechanisms aim to support regulatory compliance, risk auditing, and 
institutional trust. 
The proposed framework therefore advanc es financial AI along three 
interconnected dimensions: 
1. multimodal belief construction through integrated perception and retrieval, 
2. sequential decision optimization via reinforcement learning over belief states, 
and 
3. cross-modal interpretability at both representation and policy levels. 
By unifying perception, reasoning, and decision within a coherent architecture, this 
study seeks to establish a foundation for next-generation financial decision systems that 
are risk-aware, interpretable, and deployable in real-world markets. Beyond investment 
applications, the framework holds potential implications for regulatory technology 
(RegTech), systemic risk monitoring, and financial inclusion by enabling more 
transparent and reliable AI-driven financial analysis. 
2. Literature Review 
Deep learning has been widely adopted in financial forecasting, particularly for 
modeling nonlinear temporal dependencies in stock prices and market indicators. 
Recurrent neural networks (RNNs), especially Long Short -Term Memory (LSTM) 
networks, have becom e a standard approach for capturing sequential patterns in 
financial time series. LSTM -based architectures have demonstrated superior 
--- PAGE 3 ---
performance over traditional statistical models by effectively modeling long -term 
dependencies and nonlinear dynamics in stock price movements [15], [16]. 
Several studies have further integrated convolutional neural networks (CNNs) with 
LSTM models to enhance predictive performance. In these hybrid architectures, CNN 
layers are employed to extract local spatial features from price matrices or technical 
indicators, while LSTM layers capture temporal evolution [17], [18]. Empirical 
evidence suggests that CNN –LSTM and CNN –BiLSTM models often outperform 
standalone LSTM or CNN models in stock price prediction and volatility forecas ting 
tasks [19]. These approaches highlight the importance of combining spatial feature 
extraction with temporal modeling in financial forecasting. 
However, despite their predictive effectiveness, most LSTM - and CNN -based 
financial models rely primarily on  numerical price data and technical indicators, 
neglecting textual and contextual information that may significantly influence market 
behavior. 
 
2.1 Transformer-Based and Vision-Based Models in Finance 
With the emergence of Transformer architectures, attention  mechanisms have 
increasingly been applied to financial time -series analysis. Transformer-based models 
offer advantages in modeling long -range dependencies without recurrence, making 
them suitable for capturing complex financial dynamics [20], [21]. 
In parallel, Vision Transformer (ViT) architectures have been explored in financial 
contexts, particularly for processing chart -based representations of time series. By 
converting price sequences into image -like representations, ViT models can capture 
global str uctural patterns in technical charts [22]. Hybrid architectures combining 
CNNs with Transformer modules have also been proposed, leveraging CNNs for local 
feature extraction and Transformers for global context modeling in stock prediction 
tasks [23]. 
While these approaches improve representation learning, they largely remain 
within single -modality frameworks —either numerical sequences or visual chart 
representations. The integration of heterogeneous modalities, including textual reports, 
analyst commentary, and structured financial documents, remains limited. 
 
2.2 Reinforcement Learning in Financial Decision-Making 
Beyond prediction, reinforcement learning (RL) has been extensively investigated 
for financial decision -making tasks, including portfolio allocation,  trading strategy 
optimization, and risk management. Early work applied Deep Q-Networks (DQN) and 
policy gradient methods to learn trading strategies directly from historical price 
sequences [24]. Subsequent research introduced actor –critic frameworks such  as 
--- PAGE 4 ---
Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and Asynchronous Advantage Actor –Critic 
(A3C), demonstrating improved stability and performance in dynamic portfolio 
management [25], [26]. 
More advanced models have incorporated graph neural networks within RL 
frameworks to model inter-asset correlations and improve diversification strategies [27]. 
These RL-based approaches move beyond static prediction toward adaptive decision 
optimization under uncertainty. 
Nevertheless, existing financial RL models typically define  the state space using 
historical prices and technical indicators alone. They rarely incorporate multimodal 
contextual information such as earnings reports, macroeconomic signals, regulatory 
disclosures, or visual chart structures. As a result, the learned policies may fail to fully 
reflect the semantic and structural complexity of real-world financial markets. 
 
2.3 Large Language Models in Finance 
The rapid advancement of large language models (LLMs) has significantly 
reshaped research in financial natural  language processing. Domain -adapted models 
such as FinBERT [4] and FinancialBERT [5] have been widely applied to sentiment 
analysis of financial news, earnings call transcripts, and event -driven market 
interpretation tasks. These models demonstrate improv ed performance over general -
purpose language models by incorporating financial -specific corpora and domain 
vocabulary. 
More recently, large -scale foundation models trained on financial data, such as 
BloombergGPT [28] and FinGPT [29], have further extended the application scope of 
LLMs in finance. These models support tasks including document summarization, 
financial question answering, event extraction, and regulatory document interpretation. 
Their performance indicates that domain adaptation at scale can s ubstantially enhance 
contextual understanding and factual consistency in financial texts. 
In parallel, retrieval -augmented generation (RAG) frameworks have been 
introduced to mitigate hallucination and improve factual grounding in LLM -based 
systems [30]. By dynamically retrieving external financial documents —such as 10-K 
filings, macroeconomic reports, or policy announcements —RAG-based models 
improve reliability and contextual completeness. Recent multimodal RAG systems 
tailored to financial question answer ing further demonstrate the feasibility of 
integrating structured tables, textual disclosures, and visual information into unified 
answer-generation pipelines [31], [32]. 
Despite these advances, the majority of LLM -based financial applications remain 
focused on descriptive and analytical tasks. Current systems are primarily designed for 
summarization, information retrieval, and text -based reasoning rather than sequential 
--- PAGE 5 ---
financial decision-making. In other words, LLMs in finance have largely functioned as 
advanced information processors rather than decision policy optimizers. 
Furthermore, LLM -based systems are typically developed independently from 
reinforcement learning frameworks used for portfolio management and trading strategy 
optimization [33]. As a result, multimodal financial understanding and decision policy 
learning are treated as separate research directions, lacking a unified architecture that 
integrates semantic belief construction with sequential optimization. 
These limitations suggest that the next stage of financial AI development lies not 
merely in improving language understanding or multimodal reasoning accuracy, but in 
transforming LLM-derived multimodal representations into structured belief states that 
can support risk-sensitive decision optimization. 
 
2.4  Research Gap 
The existing body of literature reveals substantial progress across multiple research 
streams in financial artificial intelligence. Deep learning models such as LSTM-, CNN-, 
and Transformer -based architectures have improved pred ictive performance in 
financial time -series modeling. Vision -based models, including CNN and Vision 
Transformer (ViT) frameworks, have demonstrated effectiveness in extracting 
structural information from technical charts. Reinforcement learning methods hav e 
enabled adaptive portfolio management and trading strategy optimization. Meanwhile, 
large language models and retrieval -augmented systems have enhanced financial text 
understanding and document-level reasoning. Despite these advancements, a structural 
fragmentation persists. 
First, multimodal financial representation learning and sequential decision 
optimization are largely developed in isolation. MLLM-based systems focus primarily 
on information synthesis, summarization, and question answering, whereas 
reinforcement learning frameworks concentrate on action optimization using low -
dimensional numerical states such as historical prices and technical indicators. The 
absence of a principled mechanism for transforming multimodal semantic 
representations into structured decision states limits the integration of perception and 
policy learning. 
Second, existing financial RL models typically define the state space using 
numerical time-series features, neglecting rich contextual signals embedded in textual 
disclosures, earnings reports, regulatory filings, and visual chart structures. As a result, 
policy learning is often decoupled from higher -level semantic understanding, 
potentially leading to suboptimal or context-insensitive strategies. 
Third, interpretability re search in financial AI remains modality -specific. While 
token-level attribution methods and Grad -CAM visualizations provide local 
--- PAGE 6 ---
explanations, they do not extend naturally to policy -level decision attribution in 
sequential settings. The interpretability o f reinforcement learning policies under 
heterogeneous multimodal inputs remains underexplored. 
Taken together, these observations indicate a critical gap: 
There is currently no unified financial AI framework that integrates multimodal 
belief construction, risk-sensitive reinforcement learning, and cross -modal decision -
level interpretability within a coherent sequential decision architecture. 
Addressing this gap requires reconceptualizing multimodal large language models 
not as end-task predictors, but as belief constructors that generate semantically enriched 
state representations. These belief states must then be connected to reinforcement 
learning agents capable of optimizing policies under uncertainty and risk constraints. 
Furthermore, decision -level attr ibution mechanisms are necessary to ensure 
transparency and regulatory compatibility in high-stakes financial environments. 
The proposed research aims to fill this gap by developing a decision -centric 
multimodal financial framework in which multimodal perception, retrieval grounding, 
policy optimization, and interpretability are integrated into a unified architecture. 
 
2.5 Proposed Framework and Contributions 
To address the aforementioned research gap, this study proposes a decision-centric 
financial AI architecture, termed: “ A Multimodal Retrieval -Augmented Financial 
Decision Framework with Reinforcement Learning.” 
The core innovation of the proposed framework lies in the integration of multimodal 
belief construction, sequential policy optimization, and cross-modal interpretab ility 
within a unified system. 
First, multimodal large language models (MLLMs) and retrieval -augmented 
generation mechanisms are conceptualized as market belief constructors. Rather than 
serving as end -task predictors, these componen ts synthesize heterogeneous financial 
signals—including textual disclosures, technical charts, numerical indicators, and 
retrieved external documents—into structured latent representations. These belief states 
encode semantically enriched market conditions  and provide a high -dimensional yet 
interpretable representation of financial environments. 
Second, reinforcement learning (RL) is introduced as the decision optimization 
layer. Operating over multimodal belief states, the RL agent learns risk -sensitive 
policies for portfolio allocation and sequential financial decision -making. This design 
bridges perception and action, enabling adaptive strategies under uncertainty rather 
than static analytical outputs. 
Third, the framework incorporates cross-modal interpretability mechanisms at both 
representation and policy levels. In addition to token -level and visual attributions, 
--- PAGE 7 ---
decision-level attribution techniques are employed to quantify how multimodal inputs 
influence policy outputs. This ensures transparency, acc ountability, and regulatory 
compatibility in high-stakes financial contexts. 
By integrating multimodal perception, retrieval grounding, reinforcement learning, 
and decision-level interpretability, the proposed framework advances financial AI from 
isolated analytical modules toward coherent, decision -oriented systems capable of 
operating in dynamic market environments.

4. Anticipated Challenges and Potential Solutions 
Implementing a multimodal financial AI system that integrates MLLM, RAG, and 
Reinforcement Learning (RL) presents significant technical challenges. We have 
identified four primary risks and formulated concrete mitigation strategies to ensure 
project feasibility, particularly under the constraints of single -node high-performance 
computing resources. Main challenges and potential solutions shown as table 2.
--- PAGE 17 ---
Anticipated Challenge Proposed Solution & Mitigation Strategy 
1. Computational Resource Constraints 
(Hardware Limitation) 
Training or fine-tuning Large Multimodal 
Models (e.g., LLaVA, Llama-3-Vision) 
typically requires multi-GPU clusters. 
Running MLLM inference and RL simulation 
simultaneously on a single workstation (e.g., 
RTX 5090) poses a risk of GPU memory 
exhaustion (OOM). 
(1) Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT): Instead of full-parameter fine-tuning, 
we will employ QLoRA (Quantized Low-Rank Adaptation), which freezes the 
backbone model and updates only a small set of adapter weights. This reduces VRAM 
usage by up to 60%. 
(2) Decoupled Architecture: As designed in the methodology, we decouple 
Perception (Year 1) from Decision (Year 2). The heavy MLLM is used offline to pre-
compute Latent Belief States (𝑍𝑓𝑢𝑠𝑒𝑑), while the online RL agent trains on these 
lightweight vectors, ensuring computational efficiency. 
2. Instability of RL Convergence in Noisy 
Environments 
Financial markets are characterized by low 
signal-to-noise ratios. Directly mapping high-
dimensional multimodal inputs to trading 
actions often leads to non-convergence or the 
learning of trivial policies (e.g., always 
holding cash). 
(1) Frozen Belief State Pre-training: By using the frozen 𝑍𝑓𝑢𝑠𝑒𝑑 from Year 1, we 
reduce the dimensionality of the state space for the RL agent, stabilizing the learning 
process. 
(2) Curriculum Learning: We will implement a curriculum learning strategy, starting 
the RL agent in simplified market environments (e.g., low volatility periods) and 
gradually introducing complex scenarios (e.g., market crashes) to improve robustness. 
(3) PPO Clipping: Utilizing PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization) ensures that policy 
updates are bounded, preventing catastrophic forgetting during training.

3. RAG Hallucination & Irrelevant

The RAG module might retrieve irrelevant
